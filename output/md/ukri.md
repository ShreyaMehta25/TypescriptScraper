Aim The core aims of this consortium are to: develop novel, engaging and dynamic methods which support police and their partners to gain real time feedback on trust and confidence test new and innovative ways of improving trust and confidence in the police Scope As part of the Safer Streets Research and Development Missions Accelerator Programme (R&D MAP), UK Research & Innovation (UKRI) is launching a funding opportunity focused on understanding trust and confidence in policing in the UK. The aim is to develop new, engaging and dynamic ways of measuring communities’ views, which address gaps in current approaches and remain affordable for police and their partners. Public trust and confidence in policing have declined in recent years, thought to be driven by a range of factors. Strategies, such as the Safer Streets Mission and the Police Reform White Paper, have been introduced to address this. Currently trust and confidence in policing in England and Wales is measured through several valuable tools, including: Crime Survey for England and Wales (Office for National Statistics) the Public Perceptions Tracker (Independent Office for Police Conduct) Police and Crime Commissioner Local Crime Surveys However, while the currently available tools provide a snapshot of public trust and confidence, they do not provide continuous measurement or at a level of granularity to consistently understand the impact of specific policing activities and interventions. Further, the voices of less heard groups and communities, such as children, victims and survivors of abuse, and justice involved individuals, can be missing through conventional methods of data collection, limiting the representation included in the data. The challenge aims to address this limitation by investing in the development of new standardised measures, which provide police with real-time data on trust and confidence in their communities, to guide their interventions and activity. The measures will use evidence based, innovative methods to engage less heard groups, including children, and victims of abuse. To ensure that outputs are useable by police to guide their activities and interventions, they must be affordable, scalable and appealing to the user. Further, the challenge aims to increase trust and confidence through pilots in at least four areas which identify and test specific policing interventions or approaches. In combination with the measures, this will build understanding of what works well and less well in building and sustaining trust and confidence. This could include testing the use of technologies, volunteer police, community engagement and public contact methods, understanding the impact of social media campaigns, or piloting new tactics or protocols with ethical policing at their centre. Under this funding opportunity, one Research & Innovation Consortium will be established. The consortium will bring together academics (including those with research, lived experience or both, within relevant communities), police, community safety and justice leads, policymakers, third sector organisations and community groups, and relevant industry partners, to co-produce innovative, practical and inclusive measures of confidence and trust in policing. The consortium will engage with police and other organisations to design and implement a series of pilots intended to increase trust and confidence. The impact of these will be tested through the innovative measures. The consortium must prioritise understanding and capturing the experiences and perceptions of: children under the age of 18 victims of violence against women and girls The consortium should also include other underrepresented groups, prioritised with their local forces, and detail and justify their selection. However, it is expected that the measures will be able to provide a comprehensive and dynamic indication of trust and confidence in policing across the British public more generally as well. The consortium may draw on a range of technologies, which can include social media. The tools and approaches developed should be easy to use, affordable, accessible to a wide range of groups, and capable of capturing real-time data. Outputs The consortium is expected to have a core team responsible for delivering its own work packages, but a key feature of the R&I Consortium is its devolved funding pot – a ring-fenced allocation of funding designed to support small-scale pilot projects that test novel, high-risk innovations and that engage with communities. This flexible funding enables rapid experimentation, encourages bold ideas, builds a larger community of practice, and helps surface approaches with strong potential to scale. Through this structure, the consortium will design and manage workstreams to: provide an initial scoping phase in which proposed measures which intend to address the challenge will be evaluated. The most promising options will be selected develop tools to address the challenge based on the scoping phase findings trial and test the tools against real world policing interventions and activity, to identify what works well and less well in building and sustaining confidence and trust, for example, the local use of technologies, volunteers and communication, or the impact of social media campaigns. The areas for investigation will be prioritised with local forces and stakeholders refine the tools based on trial and test performance integrate the findings of the trial and test data, with the performance of policing interventions and activity to contribute to a Public Confidence in Policing Playbook. Throughout the duration of funding the consortium will engage with the UKRI Safer Streets team and associated governance mechanisms, including (but not limited to) the Safer Streets Mission Programme Board. Timescales The consortium must be ready to commence projects from 1 February 2027. Development of tools and interventions, available for piloting by December 2027. Trial testing and refinement by March 2029. Contribution to development of Public Confidence in Policing Playbook by December 2029. Project completion by March 2030. What is a Research & Innovation Consortium? A R&I Consortium brings together communities from across academia, public sector at various scales, policing, industry and the third sector, with each partner contributing distinct expertise, experience and relationships. Working collaboratively, members of the consortium cocreate tangible, creative and challenge focused outcomes that address a defined issue and results in real-world impact. Collaboration To realise the ambitions of this funding opportunity, projects are expected to set out clear plans for engaging and actively collaborating with relevant stakeholders. You should identify the most relevant stakeholders and describe appropriate and meaningful engagement plans. Potential stakeholders may include, but are not limited to, the police, third sector, community groups, industry, the public sector, people with lived experience and the public. By people with lived experience and the public, we mean both individuals and the groups or organisations that support, work with, facilitate engagement with or represent people with lived experience and the public. Projects should demonstrate how they will work with the relevant research users and stakeholders they have identified to shape, refine and deliver their work. Engagement should ensure that resulting outcomes are meaningful, usable and accessible to those audiences. This is essential to ensure that the consortium can make a substantive contribution to the development and implementation of measurement tools and approaches. As the consortium is required to deliver at least four pilots with UK police forces, applicants must demonstrate a track record of collaboration with police forces. Such collaborations should be established by the point of application submission and evidenced by project partner letters of support. Where formal partnerships are not yet in place, applicants must provide a credible plan demonstrating their ability to rapidly develop relationships and secure police partners if successful. Organisations, police forces and partner agencies that are not included in the successful application will not be excluded from future involvement. As the consortium develops, opportunities for involvement will be available through the flexible fund, as well as through additional pilots, scaling activities and wider implementation and roll-out efforts. Collaboration in practice The consortium’s core team should include project co-leads from UK-based research organisations eligible for UKRI funding, and project co-leads from eligible UK-based businesses, third sector organisations and government bodies, and could also include international project co-leads, if relevant. Representation and inclusion of specialist expertise, drawn from across sectors, disciplines and communities, are vital to support the desired outcomes. The core team should include people who collectively offer: core social science leadership, with an in-depth understanding of relevant disciplines, policy challenges and evidence needs thematic expertise, place-based expertise or both, beyond the social sciences and beyond academia, reflecting the communities that will be engaged in the consortium (including people with lived experience) an ability to engage and collaborate credibly with diverse stakeholders and research users through a range of activities and outputs an ability to bring together diverse expertise within projects, conceptually, methodologically and theoretically an ability to deliver complex projects to time, considering the variety of activities and outputs specialist expertise including programme and project management, finance expertise, a communications function, administrative support and knowledge mobilisation expertise Applications must include a co-design plan demonstrating how police and members of the target underrepresented communities have been, or will be, involved in designing the tools or approaches before deployment. Co-design must be genuine and documented, and the application must describe the specific design decisions that co-design will inform. The partnerships formed and research undertaken by the consortium should not be extractive. The consortium must take a transparent, mutually beneficial approach in which the perspectives and priorities of seldom heard communities are respected and meaningfully integrated into outcomes. Outcomes must therefore work for both the community, and the police. The consortium should articulate its ambition to develop trusted relationships with the communities and stakeholders it engages with. This includes demonstrating how trust and confidence in the project team, the research process, and the proposed co-design activities will be established from the start and maintained throughout. What we're looking for Demand management Demand management is not being applied to this funding opportunity Aim UKRI is seeking to support research that can help develop consensus estimates of the economic and social return on investments in research and development (R&D). This funding opportunity aims to spur rigorous, empirical studies that can narrow the range of existing estimates and inform public and private R&D investment decisions. This funding opportunity is solely supported by UKRI but has been developed in collaboration with the Alfred P. Sloan Foundation and Coefficient Giving as part of an international research challenge. UKRI award holders will be encouraged to engage with award holders funded directly by the Alfred P. Sloan Foundation and Coefficient Giving. Scope This funding opportunity is supported through the UK Metascience Unit. Metascience is a rapidly expanding research field that draws on a wide range of disciplinary expertise to understand how research is conducted, funded and supported, and how these practices can be enhanced or improved. For a deeper understanding of what metascience means to UKRI, please see the UK Metascience Unit’s report. Coined after economist Zvi Griliches, ‘the Griliches Question’ explores the social and economic return on investment in R&D, a measure that has significant policy implications. The UK Government’s science, innovation and technology R&D budget will grow in real terms over the Spending Review period, totalling £58.5 billion from 2026 to 2027 to 2029 to 2030, with the current best estimate being that, on average, for every £2 of private R&D investment, £8 of net benefits is generated in the long run (DSIT R&D plans 2029/2030). Despite a substantial body of evidence on R&D returns, important questions remain about how best to measure these returns and how they vary across sectors, places, technologies, and funding mechanisms. This programme supports innovative and ambitious research projects to deepen our understanding and improve accuracy in measuring the return on investment in R&D. Given the importance of quantitative evidence in informing UK policy and investment decisions, proposals are encouraged to develop robust estimates and empirical approaches that can strengthen understanding of R&D returns and support evidence-based decision making. However, applicants may also draw on qualitative and mixed methods approaches where these can help address key evidence gaps, explain underlying mechanisms, contextualise quantitative findings, or contribute to a richer understanding of how R&D generates social and economic benefits. This funding opportunity aims to generate evidence that can support policymakers, funders and investors in making more informed decisions about the scale, targeting and allocation of future R&D investments. Some questions that can be explored through this grant, but are not limited to include: Estimating returns how the return to R&D should be defined for the purpose at hand, including private vs. social returns and marginal vs. average returns, and which empirical objects best map these concepts what the returns to public spending are and how these vary across different funding designs what the social returns to corporate R&D are, including whether there is evidence of differential returns to applied research and development activities the extent to which public and private R&D investments act as substitutes or complements Spillovers, welfare and policy how knowledge spillovers flow across firms, sectors, and borders and whether new data sources or methods can improve our ability to trace them the rate at which knowledge produced by R&D becomes obsolete and whether this varies across fields how large the welfare gains from R&D are in domains where benefits are poorly captured by GDP how public policy (e.g. tax incentives, program design, subsidies) affects the level and productivity of R&D investment Methodology what accounts for the wide range of published estimates, and what refinements or reconciliations across approaches might narrow disagreement? what approaches to decomposing returns across sectors, technologies, and investment approaches can provide decision-relevant insight on the various sources of overall returns? how productivity impacts can be measured in ways that are robust to alternative specifications and research contexts? how methodologies can better capture R&D activities, sectors, places and impacts that may be overlooked in existing evidence, datasets and measurement approaches Actionable insights for policy and wider public sector organisations should be a goal of the projects awarded through this programme. Administrative data can support analysis of economic outcomes (including productivity, earnings, employment and business growth), as well as wider public sector, health, social and distributional impacts. Researchers should ensure their research questions align with available data sources. In particular, you may wish to consider Administrative Data Research UK (ADR UK), an ESRC-supported UK-wide partnership that is transforming access to public sector data for research in the public interest. Potentially relevant datasets include: business datasets, such as the Inter-Departmental Business Register (IDBR) and Business Enterprise Research and Development (BERD) data, which may support analysis of productivity, innovation activity, firm growth and business performance earnings and employment datasets, including ADR UK flagship datasets such as Annual Survey of Hours and Earnings (ASHE) linked datasets, which may support research into labour market returns, earnings outcomes, workforce composition and the distribution of economic benefits other ADR UK flagship datasets, including Longitudinal Education Outcomes (LEO) and RAPID: Population interactions with employment, benefits and pensions, which may support research into wider returns through skills development, employment pathways, earnings, employment participation and broader measures of social welfare Access to administrative data is subject to researcher and project accreditation. Aim Quantum Mission 1 calls for three core outcomes: a competitive UK quantum computing ecosystem demonstrable economic and societal value widespread adoption and integration Successful applications will contribute towards the delivery of transformative applications that create clear economic and societal value beyond classical computing. This will be delivered through application-driven methodology to develop end-to-end hybrid workflows. Scope Quantum Mission 1 is part of the UK’s National Quantum Strategy and it calls for a globally competitive quantum computing ecosystem capable of delivering quantum computers that exceed one trillion coherent operations well in excess of classical capabilities. It aims to combine this capacity with demonstrable economic and societal benefits to enable widespread adoption and integration within classical workflows. As set out in the independent review of the Future of Compute, quantum computing will play an important part in UK’s high-performance computing ecosystem, where quantum-classical solutions are likely to be critical to achieving best results. Furthermore, cloud access and orchestration of resources has the potential to make quantum computing more accessible to a broad range of users. Mission success will require: understanding of the quantum sectors’ need for advanced computing capabilities addressing critical quantum-classical integration challenges These collectively will enable development of application-ready, end-to-end hybrid workflows, supporting the effective deployment of quantum capabilities alongside HPC and AI within user-driven computing environments.
