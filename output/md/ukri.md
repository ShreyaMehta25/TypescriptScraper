UKRI encourages organisations to support applicants in preparing well-planned, high-quality applications that are competitive for funding relative to the funding opportunity Aim The core aims of this consortium are to: develop novel, engaging and dynamic methods which support police and their partners to gain real time feedback on trust and confidence test new and innovative ways of improving trust and confidence in the police Scope As part of the Safer Streets Research and Development Missions Accelerator Programme (R&D MAP), UK Research & Innovation (UKRI) is launching a funding opportunity focused on understanding trust and confidence in policing in the UK. The aim is to develop new, engaging and dynamic ways of measuring communities’ views, which address gaps in current approaches and remain affordable for police and their partners. Public trust and confidence in policing have declined in recent years, thought to be driven by a range of factors. Strategies, such as the Safer Streets Mission and the Police Reform White Paper, have been introduced to address this. Currently trust and confidence in policing in England and Wales is measured through several valuable tools, including: Crime Survey for England and Wales (Office for National Statistics) The Public Perceptions Tracker (Independent Office for Police Conduct) Police and Crime Commissioner Local Crime Surveys However, while the currently available tools provide a snapshot of public trust and confidence, they do not provide continuous measurement or at a level of granularity to consistently understand the impact of specific policing activities and interventions. Further, the voices of less heard groups and communities, such as children, victims and survivors of abuse, and justice involved individuals, can be missing through conventional methods of data collection, limiting the representation included in the data. The challenge aims to address this limitation by investing in the development of new standardised measures, which provide police with real-time data on trust and confidence in their communities, to guide their interventions and activity. The measures will use evidence based, innovative methods to engage less heard groups, including children, and victims of abuse. To ensure that outputs are useable by police to guide their activities and interventions, they must be affordable, scalable and appealing to the user. Further, the challenge aims to increase trust and confidence through pilots in at least four areas which identify and test specific policing interventions or approaches. In combination with the measures, this will build understanding of what works well and less well in building and sustaining trust and confidence. This could include testing the use of technologies, volunteer police, community engagement and public contact methods, understanding the impact of social media campaigns, or piloting new tactics or protocols with ethical policing at their centre. Under this funding opportunity, one Research & Innovation Consortium will be established. The consortium will bring together academics (including those with research, lived experience or both, within relevant communities), police, community safety and justice leads, policymakers, third sector organisations and community groups, and relevant industry partners, to co-produce innovative, practical and inclusive measures of confidence and trust in policing. The consortium will engage with police and other organisations to design and implement a series of pilots intended to increase trust and confidence. The impact of these will be tested through the innovative measures. The consortium must prioritise: understanding and capturing the experiences and perceptions of children under the age of 18 victims of violence against women and girls The consortium should also include other underrepresented groups, prioritised with their local forces, and detail and justify their selection. However, it is expected that the measures will be able to provide a comprehensive and dynamic indication of trust and confidence in policing across the British public more generally as well. The consortium may draw on a range of technologies, which can include social media. The tools and approaches developed should be easy to use, affordable, accessible to a wide range of groups, and capable of capturing real-time data. Outputs The consortium is expected to have a core team responsible for delivering its own work packages, but a key feature of the R&I Consortium is its devolved funding pot – a ring-fenced allocation of funding designed to support small-scale pilot projects that test novel, high-risk innovations and that engage with communities. This flexible funding enables rapid experimentation, encourages bold ideas, builds a larger community of practice, and helps surface approaches with strong potential to scale. Through this structure, the consortium will design and manage workstreams to: provide an initial scoping phase in which proposed measures which intend to address the challenge will be evaluated. The most promising options will be selected develop tools to address the challenge based on the scoping phase findings trial and test the tools against real world policing interventions and activity, to identify what works well and less well in building and sustaining confidence and trust, for example, the local use of technologies, volunteers and communication, or the impact of social media campaigns. The areas for investigation will be prioritised with local forces and stakeholders refine the tools based on trial and test performance integrate the findings of the trial and test data, with the performance of policing interventions and activity to contribute to a Public Confidence in Policing Playbook Throughout the project the consortium will engage with the R&D MAP steering group established for this project. The steering group terms of reference will include providing expert advice, and direction on proceeding or ceasing activity. Contribution to development of Public Confidence in Policing Playbook by December 2029. Project completion by March 2030. What is a Research & Innovation Consortium? A R&I Consortium brings together communities from across academia, public sector at various scales, policing, industry and the third sector, with each partner contributing distinct expertise, experience and relationships. Working collaboratively, members of the consortium cocreate tangible, creative and challenge focused outcomes that address a defined issue and results in real-world impact. How we will assess your application Assessment process We will assess your application using the following process. Assessing statements of interest (SoIs) The appropriate programme manager will review your SoI to ensure that the data required for its consideration is complete. They will then pass your SoI to the STFC Science Board for review. You will receive feedback on your SoI from the office of the Executive Director of Programmes on behalf of STFC. STFC aims to provide feedback within 20 working days. Any STFC Science Board member with a personal conflict of interest will withdraw from the review process for the duration of the consideration of your proposal. Panel We will invite experts to use the evidence provided by reviewers and your applicant response to assess the quality of your application and make a funding recommendation. Project management and delivery review Each proposal is also subject to a project management and delivery review. This is undertaken by a relevant project management expert, who will be given access to the proposal through the peer review extranet. The default approach is for this review to be undertaken by an appropriate member of PPRP. Where this is not possible, alternative reviewers will be sourced who will conduct the review under the same protocols as PPRP members (agreement to these protocols will be sought prior to the review being conducted). The review will be sent to applicants through Office Message Encryption (OME) by the PPRP secretariat around two weeks before the panel for the applicants to provide a response. This response should be returned by OME within five working days of receipt. In order to assist PPRP with its deliberations, the project management reviewer (whether or not a PPRP member) will attend the meeting to raise questions on the project management aspects of the proposal. You are asked to engage positively with these questions, which form a key part of PPRP’s consideration of proposals. Preliminary assessment of the proposal and resource work packages will be made by STFC staff. You will be contacted directly if there are any areas of the proposal that require more detail or rework in advance of the PPRP meeting. PPRP meeting The PPRP meeting takes place to assess the proposal and question the applicant. The meeting consists of open sessions, where you will give a presentation and members of the public can attend, and closed sessions. In its assessment of proposals, the panel will look at each category referred to within the ‘Vision and Approach’ document as well as the sections on ‘Governance’, ‘Applicant and team capability to deliver’, ‘Resources and cost justification’ and ‘Ethics’ to ensure all requirements are met. The panel also assess the reviews from external reviewers, the project management and delivery review along with the applicant’s response to them. Applicants are given the opportunity to present during the PPRP meeting, followed by question and answer sessions. If undergoing the non-light touch process (see the ‘Light touch process’ section below), feedback questions from the meeting are sent to the applicants to respond to ahead of the visiting panel. This includes requests for descopes (see the ‘Descopes’ section below). These responses are assessed at the visiting panel meeting. Visiting panel meeting The visiting panel meeting carries out a detailed assessment of the proposal. The meeting consists of panel experts and members of PPRP (a subset of those who attended the full meeting). The final recommendation usually takes place at the end of this meeting during a closed session. PPRP report A report from the visiting panel is written by the PPRP secretary in conjunction with the STFC programmes directorate and agreed and finalised by the visiting panel chair. The report presents the visiting panel’s findings and is submitted to the next STFC Science Board meeting. STFC Science Board meeting At the STFC Science Board meeting, the meeting chair presents PPRP’s recommendations. The Science Board provides strategic advice and recommendations on the proposal, which are shared with the executive board and council. Outcome of the proposal Following the Science Board’s recommendations, the STFC executive will make a funding decision on the proposal and will inform the applicant and research organisation of STFC’s decision. This will include any relevant information from the Science Board’s consideration of the proposal. On average, a successful proposal will take six to nine months to go through the process (excluding the grant being awarded). Informing PPRP PPRP will be informed of the recommendations made by the Science Board (and the actual funding decisions made by the STFC executive) at the next PPRP meeting. Light touch process PPRP has a light touch process which skips the visiting panel, with all recommendations reached within the PPRP meeting. Where this process is utilised, you will be asked by the PPRP secretariat to respond to clarification questions generated by the PPRP assessor and panel experts ahead of the meeting. You may also be asked about additional descope scenarios. Questions will be sent by and should be responded to through OME. This is in addition to the postal expert review comments and project management and delivery review in the full process. Applicants who are subject to this process will be informed by the relevant programme manager. Descopes As an essential test of value for money, it is a key part of the PPRP process to request ‘descope’ (reduction) scenarios for all proposals. Such scenarios are requested whether or not there is sufficient budget to fund the proposal in full and are in addition to any reductions in scope made prior to PPRP (such as in the consideration of the statement of interest). You are requested to fully engage with this process in preparing credible scenarios for the cuts requested. Artificially inflating the grant or not engaging with these requests seriously runs the risk of the panel recommending cuts not in your control. Additional info Background CoSTAR is a £75.6 million investment by the UKRI Infrastructure Fund, delivered by the Arts and Humanities Research Council (AHRC). It created the first national network of R&D labs built to maintain and boost the UK’s world-leading position in the development and exploitation of creative IP. The CoSTAR Network provides facilities, technology research and a series of different academic and industry partnerships to support the continued growth of the UK’s gaming, TV, film, performance, and digital entertainment sectors by enabling the development of new products, services, or processes. CoSTAR delivers programmes direct to industry that lower the barrier of entry to R&D and the use of new and emergent technologies, providing support to SMEs and microbusinesses who would otherwise not have access to the facilities they need to experiment and scale their innovations. For more details on the work of the CoSTAR Network, please visit the website UK Research and Development Network for Creative Technology (CoSTAR Network). The Challenge The UK creative industries are a major economic asset, contributing £145.8bn in Gross Value Added (GVA) and supporting 2.4m jobs in 2024. The sector expanded by more than twice the wider economy over the longer term (2010 to 2024) and is highly innovative. The creative industries outperformed the rest of the economy in all forms of innovation. The UK ranks 6th globally in the World Intellectual Property Organisation’s Global Innovation Index and 3rd in creative outputs and the UK’s global strength in film, music, fashion and gaming underpins strong export performance, with almost £60bn in creative industries exports in 2023. The sector has the highest employment growth projection of all the Industrial Strategy sectors according to Skills England. Despite this performance, the sector faces challenges to growth including uncertainty around digital and Intellectual Property (IP) regulation, a £1.4bn finance gap constraining SME growth, particularly at the scale-up stages, and persistent regional disparities in concentration, investment, innovation and skills. Rapid advances in AI, immersive and virtual production technologies are reshaping creative production, creating productivity opportunities alongside risks relating to skills, Intellectual Property (IP) and cultural value. Environmental pressures are increasing as production becomes more compute- and energy-intensive. These pressures are particularly acute in creative technology-intensive activities, where rapid advances in AI, real-time production and immersive systems are increasing both productivity potential and the capital intensity of innovation, raising barriers to entry for smaller firms. Government policy recognises the creative industries as a high-growth priority sector. The government has also set an ambition to make the UK the best place to start and grow a ‘createch’ business, with the CoSTAR infrastructure a key component to meet this ambition. Createch Futures is designed to extend CoSTAR’s geographical and sub-sector reach to improve integration, access and commercial pathways, rather than replicating existing provision. As one of the Industrial Strategy sectors, the creative industries developed a Sector Plan setting out a 10-year strategy for growth including a focus on innovation and the role of place. This supported the Secretary of State’s priority of growth and good jobs in every place and the government’s Growth Mission. The Creative Industries Sector Plan sets an ambition to grow the sector, identifying innovation, R&D capability, skills and access to finance as critical enablers of growth. This builds on commitments in the Creative Industries Sector Deal and Sector Vision, which highlighted the need for stronger innovation infrastructure and university–industry collaboration to support technology adoption and export growth. In parallel, the AI Opportunities Action Plan recognises the transformative role of AI and the need to lead in both building and using AI to remain a world leader. Together, these national policies establish creative technology as a priority growth area requiring targeted infrastructure support. These drivers are reinforced by the evolution of CoSTAR. Two years into implementation, a review of CoSTAR identified gaps in network cohesion, business-facing access, and strategic alignment with rapidly evolving technologies such as AI, while reaffirming the importance of a nationally co-ordinated approach. At sub-national level, regional and local initiatives, including the Creative Industries Clusters Programme, the Creative Places Growth Fund, and locally led creative scale-up interventions, demonstrate sustained demand for creative R&D capability and improved commercialisation pathways outside London. Collectively, these drivers point to the need for an integrated national creative technology infrastructure that strengthens access, adoption and commercial outcomes for businesses beyond the current CoSTAR programme, providing the underpinning infrastructure to enable place-based activity. Aim The project will comprise two phases, the initial 18-month, evidence-gathering phase to provide outputs for UKRI and UK government. This will then be followed by a 42-month network phase, to draw the community together around the outcomes of the initial phase. For the first 18 months, the project will develop a coherent, whole-systems UK vision for future computing paradigms. This will position the UK to shape and, where appropriate, lead the development and adoption of transformative approaches to computation. It will extend beyond the limits of current and emerging technologies, and involves anticipating longer-term, disruptive paradigms. This will comprise identifying and prioritising the sovereign capabilities required for future resilience and competitiveness. This will include where the UK could or should be leading, collaborating, or building capability. And incorporates understanding how these developments may shape future computing ecosystems, including high-performance computing (HPC). It will also support Engineering and Physical Sciences Research Council (EPSRC) and stakeholders to develop a strategic roadmap for future computing in the UK. This will identify priority research challenges, capability and skills needs, as well as the pathways to proof of concept, and so on. In a rapidly evolving global landscape, the network phase will address fragmented activity by building a clear, evidence-based understanding of UK strengths, gaps, and opportunities across emerging paradigms, spanning materials, devices, architectures, software, and applications. Through open, inclusive and multidisciplinary engagement, the network will bring together the engineering and physical sciences research community. This will be alongside expertise from across the social, environmental and biological sciences, as appropriate. This will also include expertise from business and the wider innovation ecosystem, spanning a range of career stages and working in partnership with socio-technical and economic perspectives. The network will support a diverse and inclusive community and ensure that future computing paradigms are developed and assessed within their broader societal, economic, and ethical contexts. Scope The Network Plus will take a whole-systems, technology-spanning approach to future computing paradigms, covering everything from new architectures, hardware platforms to software engineering and distributed compute. Indeed, the scope covers the full span of application space and new modalities. For example, low power, cheap, self-powering (Bio-compatible, heat, solar, and so on), potentially disposable (biodegradable and recyclable) compute solutions, that are not necessarily high speed or have high compute power. The Network Plus will focus on anticipating and shaping developments in the five to 20-year timeframe. The network will explore areas where UK academia can pioneer innovations that are not yet market-ready but might be so in five to seven years. This might also be whilst investigating mid-term and longer-term advances, which the UK might be involved with over an eight to 20-year timeframe. The Network Plus will deliver an initial 18-month programme of activity to develop a robust evidence base and to guide EPSRC’s, and potentially UK government stakeholder’s, strategic direction for future computing in the UK. As a result, the primary audience for this work will be EPSRC and relevant stakeholders across UK government, given the sensitive nature of the evidence base. How we will assess your application Assessment process We will assess your application using the following process. Expert review We will invite experts to review your application independently, against the specified criteria for this funding opportunity. You will not be able to nominate reviewers for applications the Funding Service. Research councils will continue to select expert reviewers. Shortlisting We will review the comments and scores for each application. If your application is shortlisted, you will have 14 days to respond to reviewers’ comments. Shortlisted applications and associated comments will be sent to a moderating panel. Panel A moderating panel will consider the reviewer comments and scores, and the applicants’ responses to the reviewer comments, and will make a funding recommendation. NERC will make the final funding decision. The final funding decision for this funding opportunity will adopt a portfolio approach to select a diverse range of projects in accordance with How we make decisions; balancing the remit of funded research in such a way that is appropriate to the funding opportunity aims. Interviews will not be held for this funding opportunity. Timescale We aim to complete the assessment process within six months of receiving your application. Feedback We will provide feedback with the outcome of your application. This will be in the form of reviewer comments.
