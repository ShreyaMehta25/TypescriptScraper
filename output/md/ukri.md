Funding opportunity Funding opportunity: What Works Centre for a climate resilient UK Opportunity status: Open Funders: Economic and Social Research Council (ESRC), UK Research and Innovation Co-funders: Department for Environment, Food and Rural Affairs (Defra) Funding type: Grant Publication date: 1 October 2026 Opening date: 1 October 2026 9:00am UK time Closing date: 15 December 2026 4:00pm UK time Start application Apply for funding to establish and lead a What Works Centre for a climate resilient UK to transform how the UK adapts, and builds resilience to the impacts of climate change. It will work with stakeholders to generate, synthesise and mobilise evidence to inform and accelerate adaptation pilots, interventions and actions. You must be based at a UK research organisation eligible for Economic and Social Research Council (ESRC) funding. The maximum funder contribution of your centre can be up to £21.1 million. The grant must start by 1 August 2027. Open all sections Who can apply This opportunity is open to organisations with standard eligibility. Check if your organisation is eligible. Who is eligible to apply Project lead The project lead for this funding opportunity must be based at a UK research organisation eligible for UK Research and Innovation (UKRI) funding for the duration of the grant. Before applying for funding, please check the UKRI eligibility criteria. Project leads from the following organisations are not eligible for this funding opportunity: UK-based business, third sector or government bodies non-UK organisations The project lead must ensure that they contribute an appropriate proportion of their time to the overall leadership and coordination of the grant, in addition to any time allocated to research and knowledge mobilisation activities. Applications may be submitted jointly by more than one applicant. In such cases, one person must be regarded as the project lead taking the lead responsibility for the conduct of the project and the observance of the terms and conditions. Correspondence regarding the application and award will be addressed to the project lead only (and in the case of any grant agreement, to their research organisation). Project co-leads Your application must include at least one project co-lead from business, the third sector or government bodies based in the UK, subject to ESRC eligibility and costing rules. As an exception for this centre, you may allocate more than the usual 30% of total costs (at 100% FEC) to non-academic partners where clearly justified, and while remaining within the total budget available for this funding opportunity. The combined time commitment of research staff from non-academic partners can also exceed one year full time equivalent (FTE) for the duration of the project. This flexibility will enable the meaningful involvement of key local, regional and national stakeholders, whose participation is critical to the centre’s effective operation and capacity to deliver outcomes. If you wish to exceed the 30% cost threshold or the combined time commitment cap, you must clearly justify this in your application, specifically within the ‘Justification of resources’ section. Further details are provided in the ‘additional information’ section Scope UK Research and Innovation (UKRI), the Department for Environment, Food and Rural Affairs (Defra), Department of Agriculture, Environment and Rural Affairs (DAERA) in Northern Ireland, Scottish Government, and Welsh Government are inviting applications for an independent What Works Centre (WWC) for a climate resilient UK. The centre will work to transform how the UK adapts, and builds resilience to, the current and projected impacts of climate change. The centre will work with stakeholders to generate, synthesise and mobilise evidence to inform, accelerate and scale adaptation pilots, interventions and actions. UKRI and Defra are co-funding the centre, and devolved governments are formal partners. Context The UK is already experiencing the impacts of climate hazards, with extreme events becoming more frequent and severe than previously projected, while current levels of climate change adaptation are insufficient to respond effectively to these challenges (A Well-Adapted UK, Climate Change Committee, May 2026). By the 2050s, impacts could include thousands of extra heat related deaths each year, millions of homes at risk of flooding, major infrastructure disruption, and significant further economic and ecological damage, including risks arising from overseas climate impacts on supply chains and food prices. Clear evidence gaps on the effectiveness and scale up of adaptation interventions has been identified repeatedly by the Climate Chance Committee (CCC) in their 2021 CCRA3 Independent Assessment, the 2026 Well Adapted UK report and their assessments of National Adaptation Programmes around the nations of the UK. These evidence gaps hinder the monitoring and advancement of effective adaptation action needed to keep at pace with the intensifying impacts of climate change. The UK has committed to setting stronger measurable adaptation objectives through the Fourth National Adaptation Programme (NAP4), due in 2028. This will set out the actions government and its partners will take to improve the UK’s resilience to climate change out to 2033. These objectives will be achieved by setting clear delivery plans across government, and evidence from the What Works Centre should look to inform these plans. Significant uncertainty remains about how best to adapt, reflecting a lack of robust, decision‑ready evidence on the effectiveness of interventions. Simultaneously, there are significant opportunities for innovation and growth for businesses that develop adaptation solutions (in line with the UK Climate Adaptation Research & Innovation Framework (CARIF)). A What Works Centre is essential to close this evidence gap by testing and mobilising interventions in real‑world contexts to enable their wider adoption and scaling. A climate resilient focused What Works Centre will be the first centre of its kind in the world, with a dedicated focus on generating and synthesising evidence on the effectiveness of adaptation interventions. This work will build confidence in adaptation solutions, supporting their wider adoption and scaling, improving the value for money of interventions and enabling more intelligent, targeted policy design and more effective decision making. It is envisaged that this investment will demonstrate the UK’s global leadership in adapting to climate impacts, protecting the environment and growing a sustainable economy, building on its pioneering role in the Climate Change Act 2008. Healthy natural environment and food systems From the green and blue spaces that improve our mental and physical health to the quality of air we breathe, to the water we drink and the soil that grows our food, healthy natural environment and climate resilient food systems are essential to people’s health and wellbeing. The centre will explore adaptation interventions that most effectively reduce climate-related risks to ecosystems, land use, natural resources and domestic food production. The aim is to generate evidence and actionable solutions that restore nature through more resilient habitats, support sustainable food production and resilient supply chains in a changing climate. Example focus areas could include, but are not limited to, wildfire management, drought preparedness, reducing pressure from non-native species, pests and diseases, nature recovery, UK food production and resilient domestic food supply chains (including supply, transport and storage). Thriving places Thriving places depend on homes, buildings and community spaces that can withstand a changing climate and are adapted to minimise the impacts on health and wellbeing from climate change. The centre will explore adaptation interventions that most effectively reduce risks from extreme heat, overheating and other climate-related risks (such as flooding, coastal erosion and storm risks) in the places where people live, learn, work and receive care. The aim is to generate evidence and actionable solutions that help create safe, comfortable and climate-resilient environments across the UK. Example focus areas could include, but are not limited to, homes, public buildings, health and social care settings, educational environments, emergency service facilities, prisons and community spaces. Services and infrastructure Functioning services and infrastructure are essential to resilient communities and a thriving economy. The centre will identify, pilot and evaluate the adaptation investments, governance arrangements and approaches to managing infrastructure that most effectively maintain essential services during climate shocks and long-term climate change. The aim is to build upon evidence that helps inform actions to ensure critical systems continue to function effectively across the UK in a changing climate. Example areas could include, but are not limited to, energy, water (including water efficiency and drought resilience), transport, digital connectivity, healthcare, education, emergency response and other public services and broader infrastructure and service interdependencies. Aim The successful doctoral focal award will address the UK Government AI Hardware Plan by: training a pipeline of doctoral-level very large-scale integration chip design and advanced compute specialists, building capability in AI accelerators, hardware-software co-design and energy-efficient systems, strengthening industry-academia collaboration, and establishing a national centre of excellence in semiconductor design delivering interdisciplinary, cohort-based doctoral training in integrated circuit design, systems-on-chip, verification, and system-level capability training cohorts of doctoral researchers with deep, industry-relevant design expertise, leading to physical demonstrators such as tape-out and so on anchoring UK capability in areas critical to AI hardware and advanced compute Given the importance of this area to the UK’s Modern Industrial Strategy, sector plans and AI Hardware Plan, UKRI is making this investment to ensure graduates have the desired skills needed to support economic growth and productivity in key areas of strength in the UK semiconductor sector. Scope This funding opportunity will fund doctoral focal awards to train the next generation of UK researchers and innovators. Doctoral focal awards provide support for areas that require a concentration of studentships in combination with a highly tailored training programme. Semiconductors are a critical component of the UK’s Modern Industrial Strategy, specifically within the Digital and Technologies Sector Plan. The primary aim is to build upon the UK’s existing, globally recognised, research strengths in areas such as chip design, and application with photonic integrated circuits and compound semiconductors, rather than developing new capabilities from scratch, and to translate these strengths into industrial impact. Currently, while EPSRC provides doctoral training in other semiconductor areas, there are no doctoral focal awards specifically covering chip design. The recent eFutures UK research landscape report (PDF, 37.8MB) highlights the need for further doctoral training provision in the semiconductor sector. Furthermore, the UK Government AI Hardware Plan mandates a new doctoral focal award in chip design to strengthen the long-term talent pipeline, supporting advanced research and early-stage innovation. Remit The successful doctoral focal award will deliver interdisciplinary, cohort-based training. Your proposal must cover the following core technical competencies: preparing chip designs suitable for a wide range of AI and future compute applications, including carrying out AI workloads and inference tasks efficiently chip design expertise, including hands-on application of industry-standard electronic design automation (EDA) tools, validation, and verification methodologies. This also includes expertise in chiplets, photonic backplanes, and heterogeneous integration to push the boundaries of system-level performance a comprehensive understanding of modern compute architectures, including memory integration and emerging paradigms such as neuromorphic computing application of design principles to optoelectronics, optical interconnects, and photonic integrated circuits, taking advantage of the low power, low heat, and high-speed properties of optical backplanes. This must also include compound semiconductors for high-efficiency power electronics training on assembly capabilities, packaging, and the integration of novel substrates targeting current and future manufacturing possibilities as an additional element, you may wish to include projects exploring chip design assurance and verification to understand how chip designs map onto the actual manufactured product What is out of remit The following are out of remit: proposals that do not have chip design, systems-on-chip, verification methods and advanced compute as their primary focus research focused solely on foundational materials science without a direct application to hardware design or advanced compute software-only AI algorithm development that does not involve hardware co-design or physical implementation Match funding and industry expectations Industry partners must be integrated into the delivery of the programme through direct placements, technical enablement, and co-delivery of training. Scope The NERC urgency funding opportunity supports researchers to respond quickly to transient, unexpected environmental research opportunities created by sporadic natural occurrences such as earthquakes, droughts, floods, or ephemeral events in ecosystems. The aim of this funding is to help researchers collect valuable data at the start of the event before other events and actions affect the data collection. You can apply for urgency funding only if there is an unexpected event you need urgent data on, and if applying through other available funding opportunities would result in a missed opportunity to undertake environmental research of high scientific importance. Your project outline must be submitted within four weeks of the event start date; the event start date must be clearly evidenced. Your project must fit predominantly within one or more of NERC’s Research Areas We expect your project to last up to one year and to focus on urgent data collection and essential initial analysis only. You must have a clear strategy for taking the research forward once the urgent phase is complete. We reserve the right to limit the number of urgency funding applications considered or supported relating to the same event or location. The following are out of scope for this funding opportunity: time-limited research opportunities unrelated to a discrete environmental event, such as an unforeseen opportunity to take part in a particular experiment or programme, or the unexpected availability of staff or facilities research opportunities created by deliberate direct human actions or decisions events where planning could have allowed support via alternative funding opportunities. This can include seasonal, frequent or periodic natural events that you expect to be imminent but haven’t yet happened. This can include extreme weather conditions or volcanic eruptions operational monitoring to forecast hazardous natural events responses to unexpected research findings continuation of monitoring or other existing projects collection of data to inform urgent policy or national or international decision-making Use of novel, critical technologies in your application UK Research and Innovation (UKRI) is seeking to support researchers and innovators to develop and make use of novel, critical technologies including AI, engineering biology, future telecoms, semi-conductors and quantum technologies throughout its investment portfolio. How we will assess your application Assessment process Eligibility criteria At the point of application submission, each will be checked for the following criteria: all applicants and named staff must be eligible under the funding opportunity requirements the application must meet the aims and criteria of the funding opportunity Applications which do not meet these criteria will be rejected with feedback on why it could not proceed. We will assess your application using the following process, noting that all elements of your application form will be shared with the assessors. Expert review For AHRC led applications, we receive and assess your application on behalf of both organisations, AHRC and NSF-SBE. A decision will be made within our normal standard grant’s competition process, as outlined below, noting that NSF-SBE expert reviewers will be involved throughout the process. We will invite three experts to independently review your application against the specified criteria for this funding opportunity. Applications that receive two unsupportive scores from the first two reviews will be rejected before a third review is sourced and will not proceed to the applicant response stage. A review is considered unsupportive if it is marked as ‘not recommended for funding’ or ‘not suitable for funding’ (score one to three). If an application receives a fundable score in the first two reviews, a third review will be sourced. Applicant response If you are invited to the applicant response stage, you will have 14 days to address the comments from the three reviewers and you may submit up to 500 words for each review. Applications that receive an average review score of 4.5 or below across the three sets of reviewers’ comments will be sifted out before the applicant response stage and will not proceed to panel. The only exception to this is if an application receives an average review score of 4.5 or below but has two out of three review scores averaging 5.5 or above (for example, two scores of 6, 6 or 6, 5). In this case, the application will proceed to panel, and the applicant will be given the opportunity to respond. Panel Following expert review, panel introducers will use the evidence provided by reviewers and your applicant response to moderate the quality of your application and assign it a grade. Applications will then be assigned to one of three tiers. Funding will be allocated to the applications in tier one as a priority. Applications in tier two may be recommended for funding using partial randomisation. Both AHRC and NSF-SBE will approve the final funding decision. For more information on how we prioritise applications for funding please visit How we make decisions. How we will assess your application Assessment process Eligibility criteria At the point of application submission, each will be checked for the following criteria: all applicants and named staff must be eligible under the funding opportunity requirements the application must meet the aims and criteria of the funding opportunity Applications which do not meet these criteria will be rejected with feedback on why it could not proceed. We will assess your application using the following process, noting that all elements of your application form will be shared with the assessors. Expert review We receive and assess your application on behalf of both AHRC and the FAPESP. A decision will be made as part of our standard grant competition process, as outlined below, with FAPESP expert reviewers involved throughout. We will invite three experts to independently review your application against the specified criteria for this funding opportunity. Applications that receive two unsupportive scores from the first two reviews will be rejected before a third review is sourced and will not proceed to the applicant response stage. A review is considered unsupportive if it is marked as ‘not recommended for funding’ or ‘not suitable for funding’ (score one to three). If an application receives a fundable score in the first two reviews, a third review will be sourced. Applicant response If you are invited to the applicant response stage, you will have 14 days to address the comments from the three reviewers and you may submit up to 500 words for each review. Applications that receive an average review score of 4.5 or below across the three sets of reviewers’ comments will be sifted out before the applicant response stage and will not proceed to panel. The only exception to this is if an application receives an average review score of 4.5 or below but has two out of three review scores averaging 5.5 or above (for example, two scores of 6, 6 or 6, 5). In this case, the application will proceed to panel, and the applicant will be given the opportunity to respond. Panel Following expert review, panel introducers will use the evidence provided by reviewers and your applicant response to moderate the quality of your application and assign it a grade. Applications will then be assigned to one of three tiers. Funding will be allocated to the applications in tier one as a priority. Applications in tier two may be recommended for funding using partial randomisation Both AHRC and FAPESP will approve the final funding decision. For more information on how we prioritise applications for funding please visit How we make decisions. How we will assess your application Assessment process Eligibility criteria At the point of application submission, each will be checked for the following criteria: all applicants and named staff must be eligible under the funding opportunity requirements the application must meet the aims and criteria of the funding opportunity Applications which do not meet these criteria will be rejected with feedback on why it could not proceed. We will assess your application using the following process, noting that all elements of your application form will be shared with the assessors. Expert review We receive and assess your application on behalf of both the AHRC and FNR. A decision will be made as part of our standard grant competition process, as outlined below, noting that FNR will approve the experts shortlisted to provide a review for your application. We will invite three experts to independently review your application against the specified criteria for this funding opportunity. Applications that receive two unsupportive scores from the first two reviews will be rejected before a third review is sourced and will not proceed to the applicant response stage. A review is considered unsupportive if it is marked as ‘not recommended for funding’ or ‘not suitable for funding’ (score one to three). If an application receives a fundable score in the first two reviews, a third review will be sourced. Applicant response If you are invited to the applicant response stage, you will have 14 days to address the comments from the three reviewers and you may submit up to 500 words for each review. Applications that receive an average review score of 4.5 or below across the three sets of reviewers’ comments will be sifted out before the applicant response stage and will not proceed to panel. The only exception to this is if an application receives an average review score of 4.5 or below but has two out of three review scores averaging 5.5 or above (for example, two scores of 6, 6 or 6, 5). In this case, the application will proceed to panel, and the applicant will be given the opportunity to respond. Panel Following expert review, panel introducers will use the evidence provided by reviewers and your applicant response to moderate the quality of your application and assign it a grade. Applications will then be assigned to one of three tiers. Funding will be allocated to the applications in tier one as a priority. Applications in tier two may be recommended for funding using partial randomisation. Both AHRC and FNR will approve the final funding decision. For more information on how we prioritise applications for funding please visit How we make decisions. Timescale We aim to complete the assessment process within nine months of receiving your application. Feedback Written feedback will only be provided in the form of the anonymised expert reviews and the final grade from the panel.
